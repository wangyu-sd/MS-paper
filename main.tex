\vspace{1em}

Mass spectrometry is one of the principal technologies for discovering and characterizing small molecules in biology, medicine and the environment.\cite{Wang2016GNPS,Alseekh2021,Gentry2024} Modern LC--MS/MS experiments can survey thousands of molecular features in a complex sample, but converting fragmentation spectra into chemical structures remains a central bottleneck. This problem persists even as metabolomics expands from single-cell measurements to population-scale studies: a recent Nature Perspective identified metabolite identification as a shared challenge across these otherwise distinct regimes and as a constraint on the construction of future metabolic atlases and foundation models.\cite{Alexandrov2026Perspective} Spectral-library matching provides strong evidence when an authentic reference has already been measured, yet libraries cover only a small fraction of the chemical diversity encountered in untargeted experiments.\cite{daSilva2015,Wang2016GNPS} Most measured spectra therefore lie somewhere between partial structural information and unresolved chemical identity.

Computational approaches have progressively improved the molecular hypotheses available for these spectra. Fragmentation trees, molecular fingerprints and formula-aware models enabled increasingly powerful database retrieval and chemical-class inference;\cite{Duhrkop2015,Duhrkop2019,Duhrkop2021,Hoffmann2022,Xing2023,Goldman2023} forward models learned to reproduce spectra and local fragmentation behaviour from molecular structure;\cite{Wang2021CFMID,Young2024,Nowatzky2025} and de novo generators increasingly construct complete, chemically plausible molecular structures directly from spectra.\cite{Stravs2022,Bohde2025,Wang2026MetGenX} Most recently, DeepMet showed that chemical language models can anticipate previously unrecognized mammalian metabolites and combine a learned molecular prior with predicted MS/MS spectra to prioritize candidate structures in complex biological samples.\cite{Qiang2026DeepMet} At the same time, representation learning on millions of unlabelled spectra has expanded the scale at which chemical similarity can be organized.\cite{Bushuiev2026Dreams} Together, these advances have substantially improved the quality and breadth of the molecular hypotheses that can be brought to an unknown spectrum.

A better hypothesis, however, is not necessarily an easier hypothesis to verify. Contemporary structure-discovery workflows have already exposed this distinction: many computational strategies can associate structures with mass-spectrometric observations, yet determining which of several compatible structures is actually correct remains difficult.\cite{Gentry2024} An implausible decoy may be excluded by many obvious spectral inconsistencies, whereas a same-formula isomer sharing most local substructures may reproduce the dominant features of the same spectrum. This distinction separates three questions that are often treated as interchangeable. \emph{Spectral reproduction} asks whether a molecule can generate a spectrum resembling the observation. \emph{Chemical support} asks whether an observed fragment mass is structurally supported and, at higher authority, whether that support can be grounded in an auditable fragmentation path. \emph{Structural discrimination} asks whether that observation supports one molecular hypothesis more strongly than the relevant alternatives. A plausible molecular structure is therefore not an elucidated molecular structure: structure generation proposes hypotheses, whereas structure elucidation requires evidence that can falsify their competitors.

Here we show that increasingly faithful molecular hypotheses expose a systematic evidence bottleneck in tandem-mass-spectrometry structure elucidation. We developed ORBIT-MS to treat discriminative fragmentation evidence, rather than molecular generation alone, as a computational object that can improve as the hypothesis space becomes harder. ORBIT-MS first asks which experimentally observed fragment masses are supported by each candidate and which of those masses distinguish close alternatives; atom-traceable fragmentation trajectories then provide higher chemical authority and provenance for selected evidence. The system separates experimental evidence from chemical plausibility, adapts its evidence model when new candidate populations expose recurrent failures, and returns bounded structural hypotheses when the available spectrum does not justify a unique answer. This framing connects two concerns that have become increasingly visible at the frontier of metabolomics---the rapid improvement of molecular hypothesis generation and the persistent difficulty of establishing chemical identity---by asking a more specific question: \emph{what experimental evidence makes one plausible molecular hypothesis more credible than its alternatives?}

\section*{Better molecular hypotheses create an evidence bottleneck}

Contrary to the expectation that better molecular generators should monotonically simplify structure elucidation, we found that structural discrimination became harder once incorrect candidates approached the true molecule. Across \result{METEOR generations and independent peer-reviewed external candidate sources}, the nearest incorrect candidates became progressively more similar to the ground truth, with increasing frequencies of same-formula, shared-scaffold and local-connectivity alternatives. This change in candidate quality created a different inference problem: spectra that readily excluded early decoys often no longer contained obvious evidence for separating the later alternatives.

\section*{Discriminative fragments resolve close molecular alternatives}

The evidence bottleneck suggests that the value of a fragment cannot be judged only by whether it can be generated or mass-matched. A molecular structure defines a broad set of fragment masses that it can support under the current fragmentation model, but many of these masses are shared by close alternatives and therefore carry little structural information. We therefore separate three levels of evidence. \emph{Fragment-mass support} asks whether a candidate can support an observed mass under the frozen structural/chemical model. \emph{Candidate specificity} asks whether that support exceeds the support available to competing structures. \emph{Chemical authority} asks whether a high-value mass assignment can additionally be grounded in an atom-traceable fragmentation trajectory. In intuitive terms, the discriminative value of a peak is determined first by how candidate-specific its supported mass is; mechanistic provenance determines how strongly that support can be interpreted chemically. \result{Insert real-spectrum fragment-mass support and same-formula discrimination result.}

\section*{Experimental spectra distinguish evidence from chemical plausibility}

A chemically plausible explanation is not necessarily experimental evidence. Structure-derived fragmentation rules can produce convincing mechanistic possibilities even when the measured spectrum is absent, and a structurally supported fragment mass may be shared by every candidate under consideration. We therefore asked how much of the apparent molecular explanation came from chemical prior and how much depended on the identity of the experimental spectrum.

We created matched structure-only and spectrum-conditioned views of the same fragmentation proposal graph. The structure-only view contained the chemical possibilities generated by the current reaction backend but no fabricated peak-presence, intensity or empirical-reactivity labels. The spectrum-conditioned view retained the same chemical prior and linked it to the measured MS/MS through a deterministic mass gate. We then evaluated three matched conditions: the correct recipient spectrum, no spectrum, and a shuffled spectrum from a distinct molecule group matched for adduct, neutral mass and \result{peak-richness/acquisition constraints}. Candidate pools remained fixed across all conditions.

\section*{Discussion}

Our results recast a central difficulty in tandem-mass-spectrometry structure elucidation. The field has made rapid progress in proposing molecular hypotheses---from formula and fingerprint inference to retrieval, spectrum simulation and complete molecular generation.\cite{Duhrkop2019,Hoffmann2022,Goldman2023,Xing2023,Stravs2022,Bohde2025,Wang2026MetGenX,Qiang2026DeepMet} Recent Nature studies expose both sides of the resulting problem: chemical language models can now generate and prioritize previously unrecognized mammalian metabolites, while structure-discovery workflows continue to emphasize that computationally compatible structures require an independent path to establish which identity is correct.\cite{Qiang2026DeepMet,Gentry2024} Our results connect these observations. As molecular alternatives become increasingly faithful, the spectral observations that once separated correct from incorrect structures become shared and non-specific. Stronger hypothesis generation therefore does not eliminate the elucidation problem; it changes its limiting step from \emph{can a plausible structure be proposed?} to \emph{what observation distinguishes it from other plausible structures?}

This shift changes what should count as useful information in a mass spectrum. Spectral reproduction, fragment-mass support and structural discrimination are related but distinct. A forward model can reproduce much of a spectrum without establishing chemical identity; a candidate can support many observed fragment masses that are also supported by every serious alternative; and an intense peak can carry little structural information if it is non-discriminative. Conversely, a weak peak can be decisive when its mass is candidate-specific, with atom-traceable chemistry supplying additional authority for how that support is interpreted. The structural value of a spectral observation is therefore contextual: it depends first on whether the mass is supported and specific to the candidate, and then on the evidentiary authority of the chemical path used to ground that support.

This evidence-centred view also imposes a useful asymmetry on scientific claims. Fragmentation chemistry is incomplete, so absence of a high-authority generated explanation cannot automatically be treated as evidence that a molecule is wrong. ORBIT-MS instead separates broad fragment-mass support from stronger mechanistic authority, privileges positive and candidate-specific evidence, and exposes unsupported regions and search limits separately. The same principle applies at the level of the final structure. A spectrum that does not distinguish two isomers should not be forced to produce one of them. By separating candidate coverage from evidence-based discrimination and by returning bounded structural sets, the method makes the resolution of the claim commensurate with the information present in the experiment.

More broadly, the results suggest a shift in how predictive models can participate in scientific inference. Metabolite identification remains a shared challenge even as the field scales towards cellular and population-level atlases and foundation models.\cite{Alexandrov2026Perspective} Increasing the number, diversity or plausibility of molecular hypotheses does not increase the information contained in a fixed experimental observation. A model that proposes a hidden state therefore answers only part of an inverse problem. Scientific elucidation additionally requires knowing which observations justify that proposal, which alternatives remain compatible with the data, and where the current evidence stops being sufficient. The natural next step is to use unresolved alternatives to identify the additional measurement most capable of separating them, turning passive prediction into sequential hypothesis testing. Regardless of whether such evidence is already available or must be acquired, the principle established here is the same: reliable molecular structure elucidation requires not only plausible molecular hypotheses, but explicit experimental evidence for why one hypothesis is supported over its alternatives.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Study design and evidentiary separation}

The study is organized around three frozen objects at each evaluation point: a candidate-generation model, a candidate-specific evidence model and the experimental spectrum. The hypothesis model maps a spectrum and precursor constraints to a candidate set. The evidence model maps the same spectrum together with a candidate set to candidate-specific fragmentation evidence and ranking. The experimental spectrum is never described as ``evolving'' unless a new measurement is physically acquired.

All population-level analyses use molecule groups, rather than individual spectra, as the primary independent unit whenever repeated spectra correspond to the same molecular structure. Development, calibration and test partitions are molecule-group disjoint. Candidate pools used for causal comparisons are frozen before evaluation, and official test data are not used to authorize model or evidence-program changes.

A chemical mechanism is considered auditable only to the level represented by the deterministic trace. Fragment-mass compatibility and reaction-level authority are therefore recorded separately: a mass match can support a candidate at the fragment-mass layer, but it is not labelled as a verified reaction without an atom-mapped event. A chemistry-backend proposal is treated as possible under that backend, not as an empirical reaction probability. Unsupported peaks are not candidate contradictions unless the relevant fragment-generation recall has independently passed a preregistered reliability criterion.

The primary endpoint is \result{frozen molecule-group discrimination metric}, supplemented by ground-truth rank, pairwise win rate, false-strong alternative rate and same-formula/near-isomer stratified performance. Global explained intensity and theoretical fragment-space size are reported as diagnostics rather than as primary structure-elucidation endpoints.

\subsection*{METEOR hypothesis model}

METEOR is the spectrum-conditioned molecular hypothesis model. The final implementation uses \result{final backbone, model size and checkpoint lineage}, with a chemistry-specific vocabulary, continuous token-mass state and peak-level spectrum conditioning. Experimental peaks are represented using absolute \(m/z\), precursor-relative \(m/z\), intensity and acquisition metadata where observed. \methodtodo{Freeze final direct-prefix/cross-attention interface and checkpoint identities.}

Training is staged: structure-only chemical adaptation; Chemical-World supervision using structure-derived fragment-reaction proposal graphs; Spectral-Reality fine-tuning using experimental spectra; and evidence-guided post-training only after the active pLSE snapshot passes the frozen reward-fidelity criteria. Generated non-ground-truth molecules may act as hard negatives or policy actions but do not become teacher-forced positive labels solely because they receive high similarity or pLSE scores.

\subsection*{Chemical-World and Spectral-Reality controls}

Chemical-World and Spectral-Reality share the same fragment-reaction graph schema but differ in experimental authority. Chemical-World contains structure-derived fragmentation possibilities only. Spectral-Reality retains the same proposal graph and links it to observed peaks through a deterministic mass gate. A peak can remain compatible with multiple fragments; the training record therefore does not force a unique fragment identity when the spectrum does not support one.

Spectrum dependence is evaluated with correct-spectrum, no-spectrum and shuffled-spectrum conditions on identical candidate pools. Shuffled donors must come from different molecule groups and match the recipient for adduct, neutral mass within \result{window}, and \methodtodo{final acquisition/peak-richness matching}. Spectral and identity lift are computed after equal-weight molecule-group aggregation.

Structured reasoning-specific controls use the parent/pilot \(\times\) normal/no-reasoning/shuffled-reasoning six-arm design. This analysis is supporting evidence and is reported in Extended Data rather than used as the principal chemical claim.

\subsection*{Reciprocal adaptation and factorial endpoint evaluation}

A reciprocal ORBIT run begins from immutable hypothesis and evidence snapshots. Each round produces a deterministic observation, selects one target, authorizes at most one bounded intervention and settles the change through paired evaluation. Evidence updates must improve the live candidate-frontier endpoint while preserving frozen anchors, non-target checks and a success bank. Hypothesis updates require a held-out molecular endpoint and correct-versus-shuffled spectrum dependence. Training loss, reward and pLSE score alone cannot authorize a METEOR promotion.

The principal causal comparison is performed after final snapshots freeze. We evaluate \((H_0,E_0)\), \((H_T,E_0)\), \((H_0,E_T)\) and \((H_T,E_T)\) on identical held-out cases and calculate the preregistered interaction effect. \result{Add fixed-side and ungated-alternation controls and replicate runs in the final experiment.} The complete cross-generation matrix is computed post hoc from frozen snapshots and is not used to tune intermediate rounds.

\subsection*{External-generator transfer}

External candidate sets are produced by \result{final peer-reviewed candidate generators/retrieval methods} without pLSE-guided sampling or reranking. All external candidates undergo the same standardization, deduplication and precursor-constraint checks. Initial and adapted pLSE snapshots are compared using paired molecule-group inference, including strict same-formula and near-isomer strata.

\subsection*{Risk-controlled structural resolution}

Candidate generation and evidence-based discrimination are reported separately. For candidate set \(\mathcal C_K\), generator recall is the fraction of molecule groups for which the ground truth is present in \(\mathcal C_K\). Conditional discrimination is evaluated only among sets containing the ground truth. End-to-end unique resolution combines both terms.

A calibration split disjoint from final testing is used to construct structural prediction sets \(\widehat{\mathcal C}_{\alpha}(S)\) under \methodtodo{final split-conformal or other frozen risk-control method}. We report empirical set coverage, candidate-set size, unique-resolution rate, conditional accuracy and false-resolution rate. A unique structure is emitted only when the calibrated set contains one candidate. Stereochemical resolution is not claimed from MS/MS alone unless independently supported.

\subsection*{Statistics}

Unless otherwise stated, the molecule group is the independent unit. Repeated spectra are collapsed within group before population-level inference. Primary effect sizes are reported with \result{95\% or 99\% confidence intervals}. Paired comparisons use \result{group bootstrap and exact/Monte Carlo sign-flip tests}, with family-wise error controlled across preregistered confirmatory endpoints by \result{final correction}. Minimum independent-group support and primary endpoints are frozen before final testing.

Acquisition strata such as instrument family and collision energy are analysed only when values are observed and source-verifiable. Default priors or missing metadata do not silently create measured strata.
