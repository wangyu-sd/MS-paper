\vspace{1em}

Mass spectrometry is the principal technology for surveying small molecules in biology, medicine and the environment.\cite{Wang2016GNPS,Alseekh2021,Gentry2024} Yet most tandem mass spectra generated by untargeted studies remain without a confident structural assignment.\cite{daSilva2015,Alseekh2021} The resulting dark metabolome is usually described by the number of unannotated features or spectra. That number is not a census of molecules. A single chemical entity can appear as multiple adducts, isotopologues, charge states, in-source fragments, collision-energy acquisitions and repeated observations across samples and repositories. Conversely, spectra that look similar can arise from different structures. The field therefore knows that dark chemistry is abundant, but not how many distinct entities it contains or how those entities are organized.

This distinction matters biologically. A feature without structure can be associated with a phenotype, but it cannot be placed reliably into a structural family, related to characterized chemistry, or compared as a chemical object across studies. Public repositories now contain enough spectra and metadata to reveal recurrent unknown signals at enormous scale,\cite{Wang2016GNPS,Bushuiev2026Dreams} and modern retrieval, molecular networking, formula prediction, learned spectrum models and de novo generators have greatly improved the hypotheses available for those signals.\cite{Duhrkop2015,Duhrkop2019,Duhrkop2021,Wang2021CFMID,Goldman2023,Bohde2025,Qiang2026DeepMet} What remains missing is a way to convert an uncertain spectrum into a structural object whose boundaries are explicit enough to support population-level counting and biological analysis.

The central obstacle is not that every spectrum must first be solved to a unique structure. Tandem mass spectra often cannot distinguish all constitutional or stereochemical alternatives, and forcing a single answer converts measurement ambiguity into annotation error.\cite{Bittremieux2023,Schymanski2014} What is needed instead is the most specific structural statement the measurement supports, together with a quantified probability that the statement contains the truth. Such a statement can be a single structure when the evidence is sufficient, but it can also be a bounded isomer set, a shared substructure or class, or only a molecular formula. If these statements can be generated reproducibly and their error controlled, they become valid units for a census even when many individual molecules remain incompletely resolved.

We developed ORBIT-MS for this purpose. Rather than treating a candidate only as a vector to be compared with a spectrum, ORBIT-MS evaluates candidate structures through a conservation-preserving fragmentation World in which atom identities, electron flow, charge, hydrogen transfer and exact mass are explicitly tracked. Spectrum-conditioned hypothesis generation supplies candidates when library retrieval is insufficient, while guided evidence search allocates computation to fragmentation paths that distinguish the remaining alternatives. Candidate recall and evidence discrimination are kept separate. The output is a surviving structural set, not a compulsory rank-1 answer.

The incompleteness of any fragmentation model makes negative evidence empirical rather than absolute. We therefore do not assume that failure to generate a peak proves a structure impossible. Instead, the stringency of candidate elimination is selected from a measured soundness--resolution curve, and the resulting confidence is calibrated on held-out chemistry. We then exploit the growth of public spectral libraries to test the frozen system on spectra that were dark at the freeze date but whose structures were independently deposited later. This prospective-by-time design asks whether confidence remains honest on chemistry that was unavailable when the system was fixed.

With this measurement layer in place, we ask a different set of questions from a conventional annotation benchmark. How many distinct structural entities does the dark metabolome contain after feature inflation is removed? Are recurrent dark entities merely a halo around characterized metabolism, or do they form remote structural families? Does biological source organize those families? And does replacing anonymous features with structural-family objects make biological signals more reproducible? These questions define the manuscript.

\section*{Discussion}

The dark metabolome is conventionally represented as a large remainder: the spectral features left after known compounds have been annotated. Our results support a different view. Once repeated ion forms and acquisitions are collapsed through bounded structural information, the dark metabolome is \result{census contraction summary} smaller than raw feature counts imply. More importantly, the recurrent remainder is not structurally featureless. It contains a near-known halo connected to characterized metabolism and \result{remote-family summary} recurrent structural families beyond that neighbourhood.

This changes the scientific object. A spectral feature is an observation tied to a particular acquisition and preprocessing pipeline. A structural entity can recur across datasets, and a structural family can be compared across biological contexts. That distinction makes it possible to ask global questions that feature-level dark metabolomics could not answer: whether unknown chemistry occupies recurrent regions of chemical space, whether biological source partitions those regions, and whether chemically related unknowns carry more reproducible biological information when analysed together.

The census depends on uncertainty being represented rather than suppressed. Only a fraction of entries are expected to resolve to a unique structure, and an even smaller fraction will carry reference-standard confirmation. The remaining entries still contribute useful structural information at the isomer, substructure, class or formula level. This is why the soundness--resolution operating point and the prospective calibration experiment are load-bearing: the atlas is useful only if a reader can distinguish a narrow, reliable statement from an unjustifiably precise one.

The results also define a boundary between the near-known and remote parts of dark chemistry. Analogue propagation, molecular networking, reaction priors and learned metabolite models are powerful precisely because much chemistry lies near what is already known.\cite{Bittremieux2023,Gentry2024,Qiang2026DeepMet} Our analysis quantifies that regime rather than arguing against it. The recurrent families beyond the predefined neighbourhood identify where reference expansion and similarity-based discovery are least informative, providing a concrete target set for future structural and biosynthetic work.

Several limitations remain. Mechanistic completeness is empirical, not guaranteed. Prospective library growth is not a random sample of all dark chemistry, because independent laboratories preferentially characterize tractable or interesting compounds. Structural-entity counts depend on validated but imperfect collapsing rules and therefore carry uncertainty. Biological-source analyses establish perturbation dependence, not direct biosynthetic origin. Family-level associations do not prove a shared biochemical pathway, and standard validation is available for only a small subset of candidates. These boundaries are retained explicitly in the released atlas.

The resulting resource is therefore not a database of asserted molecular identities. Each entry records the representative spectrum, bounded structural statement, surviving alternatives, evidence provenance, confidence, recurrence and source datasets; unresolved entries additionally record the measurement predicted to separate the remaining alternatives. In this form, the dark metabolome becomes a measurable chemical population and a prioritized experimental agenda rather than an undifferentiated annotation backlog.
